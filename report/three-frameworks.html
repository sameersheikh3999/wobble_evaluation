FICO · HOTS · TEACH — same observations, same scorer

Which rubric can AI score?

Three coaching frameworks, applied to the same three classroom observations, scored ten times each by the same model. The scorer was equally consistent on all three. What separated them was not reliability — it was whether their scales could reach the classrooms being observed.

The headline

Each framework was scored at its own proficiency bar — the level its authors call "proficient". On that basis:

The finding

HOTS awarded its top level exactly zero times in 510 judgements. Not rarely — never. Every indicator scored 1 or 2 on every observation, in every one of the ten runs.

Because HOTS is a 3-point scale whose top point is "Proficient", a proficiency verdict is unreachable in these classrooms. Its AC1 of 1.000 is not excellent reliability — it is the arithmetic of a constant. Perfect agreement on "no" every time.

Why: the scales don't reach the same places

The two frameworks fail in opposite directions, and neither failure is about the AI:

  • HOTS is pitched above these classrooms. It measures higher-order thinking — open-ended questioning, synthesis, debate, peer assessment. These were basic literacy and numeracy lessons. Its ceiling is never touched, so it cannot distinguish a weak lesson from a slightly better one at the proficient end.
  • TEACH's lower bar is too easy. Drop to "evidenced at all" and 11 of its 28 behaviours are met on every observation — the teacher treats students respectfully, uses positive language, acknowledges effort. True everywhere, so they separate nothing.

At a comparable bar, the picture inverts

Because "proficient" means different things on a 3-point and a 4-point scale, the frameworks were also scored at the one bar that means the same thing on all three: above the bottom level — did the practice appear at all?

HOTS becomes the most efficient of the three — 53% of its indicators carry signal, against FICO's 29%. The same framework that was useless at its own bar is the best-discriminating one a level down. Its problem is entirely where the bar sits, not what it asks about.

The result that did not vary

Reliability was near-identical across all three — AC1 between 0.89 and 0.92, flip rates between 3.3% and 4.7%, zero parse failures on any of them. The scorer reads all three rubrics about equally well.

So the frameworks are not distinguished by how consistently an AI can apply them. They are distinguished by what their scales can detect in the classrooms you actually have.

FICO — 10 of 33 testable work

Four of FICO's 37 were never evaluated at all, for two different reasons — listed in full in the next section.

Indicators that could not be evaluated

Four indicators produced no usable judgement in any run, and all four are FICO's. These are not low scores — they are the absence of a score, and must not be read as findings about teaching. HOTS and TEACH flagged none, but TEACH has two separate gaps the flag could not see; both are covered below.

Missing reference document — 2 indicators

Both compare the observation against the teacher's lesson plan. None of the three observations carried one, so both were excluded from scoring rather than scored. Judging adherence to a document the scorer was never shown would produce a number about a missing file.

CodeIndicatorThe bar it could not be judged against
B7Use of Taleemabad Lesson Plan "Plan followed with minor contextual adaptations."
B8Use of Prescribed Resources "Key resources used as prescribed in lesson plan."

Both become assessable the moment a plan is attached. This is a data-collection gap, not a rubric defect — and it is fixable without touching the framework.

Subject does not apply — 2 indicators

Both are subject-specific and correctly returned "not applicable" on every run, because the sample contained no maths and no science lesson. The framework behaved exactly as intended; the sample simply did not exercise them.

CodeIndicatorWhy not applicable
F5Subject-Specific Pedagogy: MATH no maths lesson among the three observations
F6Subject-Specific Pedagogy: SCIENCE no science lesson among the three observations

These would be evaluable on a sample that included those subjects. Nothing is wrong with them — they are simply untested here, and a report covering only literacy lessons should omit them rather than show them as unmet.

TEACH — none flagged, but two problems the flag missed

TEACH returned zero unassessable indicators. It needs no lesson plan — it is purely observational by design, so nothing in it compares the lesson to a document. But two gaps sit outside what that check can see, and both are mine to disclose rather than the tool's.

Two of TEACH's items were dropped from the spec entirely. Its Time on Learning section — 0.1 "Teacher provides learning activity to most students" and 0.2 "Students are on task" — is scored by timed snapshots at 4, 9 and 14 minutes, not by a single judgement over a whole lesson. A transcript scorer cannot reproduce that sampling, so I excluded them when building the spec. TEACH was therefore evaluated on 28 of its 30 items, and the two missing ones are not shown as unassessable because they were never in the run.

Several TEACH behaviours need eyes, not ears — and the automated check could not catch them, because in this conversion the criterion text sits in the behaviour's name while the L/M/H anchors are generic. The reviewer scanned the anchors and found nothing visual, which is a limitation of how the spec was built, not evidence that TEACH is fully audible.

The pattern is telling. Three of the four were scored "met" on 100% of observations — which is what you would expect when a scorer cannot see the counter-evidence: absence of audible bias is read as absence of bias. And 4.2, the one that did vary, has the worst AC1 in the whole of TEACH (0.43) — it is classified as working, but it is the least reproducible behaviour in the framework, and it is one that a transcript cannot properly evidence.

None of this makes TEACH a worse instrument. It makes it an instrument designed for a live observer in the room, being used against audio. That is a mismatch of data source to tool, and it would be resolved by video or by marking these behaviours out of scope for transcript scoring.

A judgement call worth checking

HOTS Domain 2 is called "Lesson Planning" — 2.1 Objectives Linked to HOTS, 2.2 Strategies for Analysis, 2.3 Real-World Applications. On the name alone these look like they need the plan too, and I considered marking them unassessable.

I did not, because their descriptors describe what is audible in the lesson, not what a document contains: "Objectives are explicit and linked to HOTS", "Strategies explicitly foster HOTS", "Lessons integrate real-world applications". A teacher who states an objective aloud evidences 2.1 from the transcript alone. FICO's B7 and B8 are different — they name a specific planning artefact and ask whether it was followed.

So 2.1–2.3 were scored. 2.3 works; 2.1 and 2.2 came back with no signal, scored "not evidenced" on every observation. Whether that is genuinely absent higher-order planning or an artefact of judging planning from a transcript is not resolvable from this data — it is the one place where supplying the plans would most change the answer.

The wobblers — and why none were formally flagged

The classification above shows zero NOISY indicators in HOTS and TEACH. That reads like a clean bill of health. It is not. All three frameworks carry indicators whose verdict moves between identical runs; at three observations the reliability test is simply too underpowered to certify any of them.

“0 NOISY” means “not proven unreliable”, not “proven reliable”. Two things suppress the label: the pooled binomial needs a very high disagreement rate to clear a Holm-corrected threshold at three observations, and NOISY additionally requires the indicator to pass the discrimination test. Only FICO’s C2, at a 30% flip rate, cleared both.

All three frameworks carry five or six of them, and HOTS has the worst mean flip rate of the three — 4.7%, against FICO’s 3.3% and TEACH’s 4.2%. HOTS is not steadier than FICO. The earlier finding of seven FICO wobblers came from a ten-observation study with the power to prove them; this three-observation run has no such power, and its silence should not be read as absence.

Where this bites

Three of HOTS’s five candidates — 4.1, 4.3, 3.2 — sit in the domains described earlier as “working”. Domain 4 discriminating fully is a real result; its individual indicators being stable is not established. The same caution applies to TEACH’s Critical Thinking block: 6.2 flips on 17% of runs.

Settling it means running HOTS and TEACH on the full ten observations — 600 and 300 calls — to give the reliability test the power FICO’s study had.

What I would take from this

  1. HOTS cannot be used at its own proficiency bar on these classrooms. Either lower the bar to level 2 for reporting, or accept that a "proficient" HOTS verdict is currently unattainable and say so explicitly rather than reporting 0% as a finding about teachers.
  2. Take HOTS Domain 4 and TEACH Element 6 seriously. Both are fully discriminating blocks measuring student engagement and critical thinking — the two areas where all three frameworks agree there is signal.
  3. Drop or rewrite TEACH's Classroom Culture area for this purpose. Seven behaviours, all met everywhere, no coaching signal. That may be a genuine finding about these classrooms, or a bar set too low.
  4. FICO's breadth costs it. 37 indicators but the lowest proportion carrying signal. HOTS gets more discrimination from 17.

What would change these numbers

Read before quoting

Three observations. That is the minimum for the discrimination test, and it inflates "no signal" counts on all three frameworks: an indicator must differ sharply across just three lessons to register. The comparison between frameworks is sound — they all faced the same three — but the absolute counts would rise with more observations. Do not quote "N indicators work" as a fixed property.

TEACH was scored without its manual. The PDF supplied is the observation tick-sheet: behaviour statements and L/M/H boxes, but no behaviour-level descriptors of what Low, Medium and High mean. Generic anchors were used instead. FICO and HOTS both ship full written descriptors for every level. TEACH is therefore judged on a thinner rubric than the other two, and its results should be treated as a floor rather than a fair estimate.

No lesson plans. None of the three observations carried one. See Indicators that could not be evaluated above for exactly which indicators this affected and which were judged on it anyway.

And the standing limit on all of this work: it measures whether the scorer agrees with itself, not whether it is right. An indicator that returns the same wrong answer ten times scores perfectly here. Nothing has been compared against a trained human observer.

Three coaching frameworks scored against three classroom observations — Swahili 25 min, Urdu 27 min, English 10 min — ten repeated runs each, one model, identical pipeline. 2,339 scored judgements.

Reliability is not accuracy. These figures establish which indicators an AI can apply consistently; whether it applies them correctly is a separate study.
