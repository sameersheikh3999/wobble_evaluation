Rubric reliability protocol

A repeatable way to find out which indicators in a coaching framework can actually be scored, and which cannot — before anyone builds a coaching programme on top of them. Works on any rubric: Tanzania, Pakistan, a regional variant, or one written last week.

What is being assessed

The unit is a coaching observation: an audio recording of a real classroom session, transcribed. That transcript is what the framework is applied to, and every reliability figure in this protocol describes how consistently a scorer can read it.

Some indicators need a second document. Where a teacher attaches the lesson plan she taught from, the transcript can be compared against it — did she follow it, did she use the resources it prescribed? In the framework this study used, that comparison is the job of one section (Lesson Plan Fidelity), not of the observation as a whole. The other sections judge teaching practice, student engagement and subject knowledge directly from the transcript, and need no attachment.

A mistake worth not repeating

In the study that produced this protocol, the lesson plan was never supplied to the scorer — only the transcript was. Two indicators asked whether the plan was followed and whether the prescribed resources were used. Both returned "no" almost everywhere, and one was initially classified as carrying no signal.

That classification was wrong. A scorer cannot judge adherence to a document it was never shown. The verdict measured a gap in the inputs, not a defect in the rubric or a shortcoming in the classrooms.

The protocol now treats this as a first-class case: an indicator declares the documents it needs, and when one is absent the indicator is reported NOT_ASSESSABLE rather than scored. Marking it as merely "uninformative" would quietly blame the framework for a missing file.

The question this answers

Every coaching framework is a list of indicators with descriptors. It looks authoritative on paper. But before you can use one — with an AI scorer or a human observer — two things have to be true of each indicator, and neither is visible by reading it:

  1. It reproduces. Score the same observation twice and you get the same answer.
  2. It discriminates. Different observations get different answers.

An indicator that fails the first is noise. An indicator that fails the second is a constant — perfectly stable and perfectly useless, because it tells a coach nothing they didn't already know. Both failures are common, and both are invisible until you measure.

The finding that shapes this protocol

We tried to predict which indicators would fail by inspecting their wording. It does not work. Across 37 indicators with flip rates measured over 800 scoring passes, no textual feature correlated with the wobble that was actually observed — word count, sentence count and clause count all returned |ρ| < 0.16, p > 0.36. A "two or more sentences" rule caught 4 of the 7 genuinely broken indicators at 21% precision.

Reviewing a rubric is cheap and worth doing. It is not a substitute for measuring it.

How the measurement works

The whole protocol rests on one arrangement: score every observation many times, and read the resulting grid in two directions.

Crossing the two axes gives four verdicts, and each one implies a different action:

HEALTHY

Reproduces and separates observations. Usable as-is.

NOISY

Separates observations but will not reproduce. The descriptor is ambiguous — rewrite it, or accept it needs a richer data source.

UNINFORMATIVE

Reproduces perfectly, same answer on every observation. No coaching signal. Either the bar is set outside the range of these classrooms, or the evidence isn't in your data.

BROKEN

Neither. Remove it.

Both tests are Holm-corrected across the indicator set, because testing thirty or forty indicators at once otherwise manufactures significance.

Why Gwet's AC1, not Cohen's kappa

The default choice would be kappa or Krippendorff's alpha. On this kind of data they break, and the break is easy to mistake for a real result.

When an indicator is nearly always "not met" — and in practice many are — the chance-agreement term grows until the coefficient collapses toward zero even at 95% raw agreement. That is the prevalence paradox: a property of the statistic, not of the scorer. An analyst who reports kappa here will conclude the framework is unusable when it isn't.

Gwet's AC1 corrects for chance without that failure mode. The protocol reports AC1, alpha and raw agreement together, so where they diverge you can see it rather than inherit whichever one was chosen for you.

Gwet's AC1chance-corrected agreement, prevalence-robust always — this is the headline

The five steps

  1. Import the framework

    Frameworks arrive as spreadsheets — one sheet per section, one column per level. Point the importer at the folder; it detects the header row, the level columns and the section banner.

    python run_framework_wobble.py import \
       --csv-dir tanzania_csvs --out frameworks/tanzania.yaml

    The result is editable YAML. Any number of levels, any number of sections, any band thresholds — nothing is hard-coded to a particular rubric.

  2. Check the spec

    Validates structure — duplicate codes, missing descriptors, a proficiency cut outside the scale, bands in the wrong order — and separately lists descriptors worth a human read.

    python run_framework_wobble.py check --framework frameworks/tanzania.yaml

    Spec defects (two adjacent levels with identical wording, a three-word descriptor) are real and should be fixed. The advisory flags are a reading list, not a prediction — see the finding above.

  3. Plan the run

    Reports exactly what it will cost and whether the design has the power to answer the question.

    python run_framework_wobble.py plan \
       --framework frameworks/tanzania.yaml --dir Transcripts -n 10

    Fewer than 3 observations and discrimination cannot be tested at all — every indicator returns UNTESTED. Fewer than 5 runs and you only catch indicators that flip often. 10 observations × 10 runs is the tested default.

  4. Run it

    python run_framework_wobble.py run \
       --framework frameworks/tanzania.yaml --dir Transcripts \
       -n 10 --out tanzania_out --resume

    Crash-safe: scores are written after every transcript and --resume skips what's done. A transcript that fails entirely is recorded and skipped rather than taking the run down with it.

  5. Analyse — as often as you like

    python run_framework_wobble.py analyse \
       --framework frameworks/tanzania.yaml --out tanzania_out --cut 3

    Free. Move the proficiency cut, drop indicators, re-band — all without spending a further call.

Reading the output

Three tables and a headline. What to do with each:

OutputDecision it drives
headline.json Overall AC1. ≥0.80 report individual indicators; 0.67–0.80 report roll-ups only; below that, not decision-grade.
indicator_reliability.csv The 2×2 verdict per indicator. NOISY → rewrite. UNINFORMATIVE → investigate the bar. BROKEN → remove.
section_bands.csv Whether a section's High/Medium/Low band survives a rerun. An unstable section cannot be quoted from a single pass.
lesson_by_indicator.csv The per-observation detail — for chasing down why one indicator moved.
Watch the section size

Band stability depends on how many indicators a section has, not just on how good they are. A section with four indicators moves 25 points when a single verdict flips — enough to cross a band line on its own. Trimming a weak section's worst indicators can make its band less stable, not more. If a section drops below about six indicators, rebuild it rather than trimming it.

What the protocol cannot tell you

The boundary

This measures whether a scorer agrees with itself, not whether it is right. An indicator returning the same wrong answer ten times scores perfectly here.

Reliability is necessary but not sufficient. It is the cheap test, and it should come first — there is no point validating an indicator against human experts if it cannot even reproduce. But a framework that passes this protocol has been shown to be consistent, and consistency is not accuracy. The next study is a comparison against trained human observers on the same observations.

Two narrower limits worth stating whenever you report results:

  • The data source bounds the framework. Indicators resting on what a transcript cannot carry — seating, gaze, who is writing — will fail regardless of wording. That is a finding about your data, not the rubric.
  • "No signal" is a statement about your sample. An indicator that never varied across ten observations might well vary across fifty, or across a sample containing stronger practice. Report the sample, not just the verdict.

Framework-agnostic protocol for measuring rubric reliability. Scale, sections, levels and bands come from the spec file, so the same pipeline runs on any coaching framework without code changes.

Reliability is not accuracy. This protocol establishes that a framework is consistent; a comparison against expert human ratings is required before it can be called correct.
