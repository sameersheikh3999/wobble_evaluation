Does the scorer agree with itself?

We asked an AI to grade the same ten recorded lessons, over and over, using the same rubric and the same words each time. If the grade changes when nothing else has, the grade cannot be trusted. This is what we found.

The question behind the experiment

Taleemabad runs a coaching programme for teachers. A trained observer watches a lesson and rates it against 37 separate points — whether the teacher stated a learning objective, whether students talked to each other, whether the questions asked required real thinking. Those ratings become the coaching conversation the teacher has afterwards.

Doing that by hand, for thousands of classrooms, is slow and expensive. So the obvious idea is to have an AI read a transcript of the lesson and produce the ratings instead.

Before trusting that, there is a question that has to be answered first, and it is not "is the AI right?" It is something more basic: does the AI give the same answer twice? If you hand it the identical transcript on Monday and again on Tuesday and it says the teacher gave good feedback on Monday and poor feedback on Tuesday, then whatever it is measuring, it is not the lesson. A measurement that moves when nothing has changed is unusable, regardless of how sensible any single answer looks.

That movement is what we set out to measure. We called it wobble.

What we actually did

We took ten transcripts of real lessons — in Urdu, Swahili and English, running between ten and thirty-two minutes. We gave each one to the AI and asked it to grade all 37 indicators. Then we did it again. And again, ten times per transcript, with the transcript, the rubric and the instructions kept exactly the same every time.

Any difference between those ten answers cannot come from the lesson, the rubric or the question. It can only come from the scorer.

We ran the whole thing twice over, using two different grading scales, to see whether the shape of the question changed the answer's stability:

  • A 1–4 scale, the framework's own format, where 1 means not observed and 4 means highly effective.
  • A plain yes/no, where the AI simply judges whether the standard was clearly met.

That is 800 separate gradings in total. Here is what a stable indicator and an unstable one actually look like — each square is one pass over the same transcript:

The second case is the problem in miniature. A coach reading pass 2 would praise the teacher for including everyone. A coach reading pass 3 would flag it as a weakness. Both reports would be generated from the identical recording.

What we measured

Four things, each answering a different question a sceptical reader would ask.

1. Does the answer hold still?

For every indicator we counted how often a single pass disagreed with the majority of the other passes. This is the practical error rate of quoting one AI run — the chance that the report a teacher receives contradicts what the same system would have said on a rerun.

2. How reliable is each indicator, on its own?

Because we had ten lessons rather than one, every individual indicator got its own reliability score across the whole set. We used a statistic called Gwet's AC1, which runs from 0 (pure noise) to 1 (perfect repeatability). We chose it over the more familiar Cohen's kappa deliberately: when an indicator is nearly always "no" — which many of these are — kappa collapses toward zero even when the runs agree almost perfectly. That is a known flaw in the statistic, not a fact about the scorer, and it would have made good indicators look broken.

3. Does the indicator tell lessons apart?

This one is easy to overlook and turned out to matter most. An indicator that answers "no" for every lesson in the sample is perfectly repeatable — and completely useless. It carries no information a coach could act on. So alongside reliability we tested whether each indicator's answer actually varied across the ten lessons.

4. Does the headline verdict survive?

The framework converts scores into a band — High (85% or more of standards met), Medium (60–84%) or Low (under 60%). That band is the thing a coaching report actually quotes. We checked how often the band changed between passes on the same lesson.

What we found

Finding 1 · the good news

Overall, the scorer is consistent

Across all 37 indicators and all ten lessons, average reliability came out at 0.87 on a 0-to-1 scale — comfortably in the range normally treated as dependable. A single pass contradicted the majority about 5% of the time, and 79% of the 350 lesson-by-indicator judgements came back identical in all ten passes.

The overall fidelity band for a lesson held steady on 9 of the 10 transcripts. If you report one number per lesson, that number is solid.

Finding 2 · the biggest surprise

A quarter of the framework carries no signal at all

Nine of the 37 indicators answered "no" on every single lesson — all ten transcripts, all ten passes, both grading scales. Perfectly consistent, and perfectly uninformative.

They include Quality Questioning, Effective Feedback, Student Agency, Differentiation, Self & Peer Assessment, and Use of Technology. Six of them sit in Section C, the section meant to capture high-leverage teaching practices — which means half of that section discriminated between no lessons whatsoever.

Why this matters

Either the bar for these standards sits above anything happening in these classrooms, or the behaviour simply is not visible in an audio transcript. Both are fixable, but they need different fixes — and neither is visible if you only ever score one lesson, where "always no" looks like a well-behaved indicator.

Finding 3 · the weak point

One indicator is close to unusable, and we know why

C4, Equitable Participation, scored an AC1 of 0.40 on the yes/no scale — the worst in the framework — and changed its verdict on 8 of the 10 lessons. B2, Lesson Structure, was second worst.

Because we also asked the AI to explain each judgement, we can see the cause rather than guess at it. C4's standard reads: "Deliberate strategies: cold call, pair-share, name sticks. Diverse students included." That is two requirements joined into one. The passes that said yes pointed to the teacher calling on students by name; the passes that said no pointed out that those were mostly volunteers and that many children stayed silent throughout. Both readings are correct. The rubric never says which one decides — and the same split recurred lesson after lesson, in different classrooms and different languages, which is what marks it as a wording problem rather than a hard lesson to judge.

Splitting compound standards like this into separate checks is the single clearest improvement available.

Finding 4 · the reporting risk

Two sections cannot be reported from a single pass

Section B (Lesson Plan Fidelity) and Section D (Student Engagement) were the trouble spots. Their High/Medium/Low band moved between passes on roughly half the lessons.

Finding 5 · the scale comparison

Yes/no is not more reliable than 1–4

We expected the simpler question to produce steadier answers. On a 1–4 scale most disagreement is between neighbouring levels — a 3 becomes a 4 — which rarely changes what a coach does. Forcing a yes/no removes that cushion, so we predicted it would either be visibly steadier or visibly worse.

It was neither. Comparing like with like — converting the 1–4 scores into met/not-met and running identical statistics on both — the two approaches came out level, with a slight edge to the 1–4 scale.

Prediction not supported

We built the yes/no scorer on the expectation that it would produce more dependable coaching decisions. The evidence does not support that. It is reported here because it was the hypothesis under test, and it failed.

Finding 6 · a useful side effect

The AI knows which of its own answers are shaky

We asked the scorer to rate its own confidence on each judgement, defined precisely as the chance a second careful observer would agree. Then we checked that claim against what actually happened on the other passes.

It was under-confident: across 3,360 judgements it claimed 0.73 on average and earned 0.92. More usefully, the ordering held. Of the 1,854 judgements it rated 0.70 or above, 99.7% proved reproducible; below 0.60, that fell to 71%. That gives a practical shortcut — trust the confident calls from a single pass, and rerun only the hesitant ones.

What we would do with this

  • Report the overall lesson score, not Section B or D, from a single pass. The combined figure is stable; those two sections are not.
  • Rewrite the compound standards, starting with C4 and B2. Where a standard bundles two requirements, split it or state which one decides.
  • Review the nine silent indicators. A quarter of the framework is currently generating no coaching signal. Decide whether the bar is wrong or the evidence is invisible in a transcript.
  • Keep the 1–4 scale. It matched yes/no on every reliability measure and preserves more information — you can always collapse a level into a verdict later, but you cannot recover a level from a yes.
  • Use the confidence score to triage rather than rerunning everything.

What this study does not show

The central limitation

This measures whether the scorer agrees with itself, not whether it is right. An indicator that returns the same wrong answer ten times scores perfectly here. Nothing in this experiment was compared against a human expert's judgement of the same lessons, so no claim about accuracy can be made from it. That is a separate study, and it is the necessary next one.

Three narrower caveats worth stating:

  • Eight lessons, not ten. Two pairs of files turned out to be the same audio transcribed twice. We kept them, because they let us test something else: how much the verdict depends on the transcription rather than the scoring. The pairs agreed 92% of the time, and six indicators changed verdict from the re-transcription alone — instability that better AI scoring cannot fix.
  • Transcripts only. The scorer never sees the classroom. Anything a human observer would judge visually — seating, body language, who is actually writing — is invisible to it, which may explain part of Finding 2.
  • One incomplete transcript. On the yes/no run, four of ten passes on one lesson failed to return usable output, so that lesson rests on six passes rather than ten.
